Reading Responses, About Cables

  • 17 February 2026

How To See Invisible Infrastructure by Ingrid Burrington

I thought this article was pretty fascinating. I had no clue that there were competitions and community roundups to collectivize and find fiber-optic cables and other underground utilities; I think that's a really unique way to create initiative for people to both find more interest in "invisible infrastructure" and to even explore their communities. The precision in which people who have been doing this for years is really impressive, as the author states, "locators are experts in the minutiae of landscape. Variations in strains or color of grass and cracks in pavement can offer as much insight as a device equipped with Bluetooth or elaborate touch-screen interfaces." I honestly didn't know until around the past year that most of what we know as the internet and how it functions isn't really functioning in a way that's on a "cloud" (honestly if you had asked me 5 years ago how the internet works functionally, I would've probably just guessed a bunch of satellites and connections), so Burrington's article opened me up to a sect of the "Locate" world of finding fyber-optic cables that I did not know about.

The Undersea Network by Nicole Starosielski

Still can't wrap my head around how we manage to get all of those cables underground and into the ocean...

Unsurprisingly, much of what shapes the infrustructure of the underwater and underground cables is very much influenced by geopolitics, and this reading really goes into depth about it. From what I collected from the reading, it seems that the author is using this book to detail that despite how fast the internet has grown, and the means for connectivitiy across the globe have increased, the engineers and companies behind taking care of the fiber-optic cables in relation to finding ways to optimize them are all shaped by economic, geographic, and even political constraints. (and of course, in more impoverished places or places with more stricter governement regimes, this need for improvement is more stagnated or overly regulated). Cable systems that are used for 1st-world countries or countries with high economic power also tend to have their cables run through coastal towns and places, where much of their recent histories have now been very much shaped by the impact of the infrastructure. "The Undersea Network connects the evolution of cable systems with its shifting material contexts, including not only cultural practices and political formations but also atmospheric, thermodynamic, geological, and biological processes, to expose the complexity that goes into the distribution of digital media" (pg. 14).
